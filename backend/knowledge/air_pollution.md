# Air Pollution

## In one paragraph
Air pollution is the largest environmental health risk in the world. Fine particulate matter
(PM2.5) is small enough to cross from the lungs into the bloodstream. The WHO estimates around
7 million premature deaths a year from ambient and household air pollution combined, and about
99% of the global population breathes air exceeding WHO guideline levels.

## Key pollutants
- PM2.5 and PM10: combustion particles, dust, secondary aerosols. WHO guideline for annual PM2.5
  is 5 ug/m3; many large cities run 5-20x that.
- Nitrogen dioxide (NO2): mostly diesel vehicles and combustion plants.
- Ground-level ozone: formed by NOx and volatile organic compounds reacting in sunlight.
- Sulphur dioxide (SO2): coal and heavy fuel oil; also drives acid deposition.
- Carbon monoxide, benzene, heavy metals, and black carbon (which also warms the climate).

## Causes
1. Coal and diesel combustion in power generation, industry and transport.
2. Household burning of solid fuels for cooking and heating: still ~2 billion people.
3. Agriculture: ammonia from fertiliser and manure combines with NOx and SOx to form secondary PM2.5.
4. Open burning of crop residues and waste.
5. Non-exhaust transport emissions: tyre, brake and road wear, which do not fall with electrification.
6. Construction dust, and wildfire smoke that now travels across continents.

## Consequences
- Cardiovascular and respiratory disease, stroke, lung cancer, low birth weight, and increasing
  evidence of links with dementia and diabetes.
- Children in high-exposure areas show reduced lung development and measurable learning losses.
- Ozone damages crops; global staple yield losses run into the percent range.
- Economic cost: several percent of GDP in the most affected countries.

## Solutions that work
- Low-emission zones: London's ULEZ and similar schemes cut roadside NO2 sharply within two years.
- Fuel standards and scrubbers: sulphur limits on shipping fuel cut SO2 emissions dramatically after 2020.
- Clean cooking: LPG, electricity or efficient stoves deliver the largest health gain per dollar
  in low-income settings.
- Electrify buses and delivery fleets, which are disproportionate emitters per vehicle.
- Dense low-cost sensor networks plus open data, so pressure and evidence are local.
- Ban routine crop residue burning and provide affordable alternatives (mulching, baling).

## Practical advice
Check a local AQI before outdoor exercise; on high-PM days, exercise indoors. HEPA filtration
meaningfully lowers indoor PM2.5. Cooking with gas raises indoor NO2, so use extraction that vents
outside. Cloth masks do little for PM2.5; a well-fitted N95/FFP2 does.
