# Renewable Energy

## In one paragraph
Renewables passed 30% of global electricity generation for the first time in 2023, and the world
added a record of roughly 585 GW of new renewable capacity in 2024, dominated by solar PV. Solar
module costs have fallen around 90% since 2010, which is why deployment keeps outrunning official
forecasts.

## Technologies
- Solar PV: cheapest new electricity in most of the world. Intermittent but highly predictable
  on a daily cycle; pairs naturally with batteries for evening peaks.
- Onshore and offshore wind: strong complement to solar because it often peaks at night and in winter.
- Hydropower: the largest renewable source historically; flexible, but vulnerable to drought and
  with real ecological and social costs from large dams.
- Geothermal: firm, low-carbon baseload where geology allows; enhanced systems could widen access.
- Bioenergy: only genuinely low carbon with residues and waste feedstocks and honest accounting.
- Nuclear: not renewable, but low carbon and firm. Slow and expensive to build in most markets;
  keeping existing plants running is usually the cheapest clean megawatt available.

## Why costs fell
1. Manufacturing scale and a steep learning curve, especially in Chinese solar and battery supply chains.
2. Auctions and long-term power purchase agreements that cut the cost of capital.
3. Standardised, factory-built components rather than bespoke construction.
4. Policy support that created stable early demand.

## Challenges
- Grids: transmission and interconnection queues are now the main bottleneck in the US and Europe.
- Storage and flexibility: batteries handle hours, not weeks. Long-duration storage, demand
  response, hydro and hydrogen all have roles.
- Permitting: a wind farm should not take a decade to approve.
- Critical minerals: lithium, copper, nickel, cobalt and rare earths bring mining impacts and
  concentration risk. Recycling and design for disassembly matter.
- Land use and local acceptance: agrivoltaics, rooftops, brownfields and community ownership help.

## Practical advice
At home, the ranked list is usually: insulate, then switch heating to a heat pump, then electrify
transport, then add solar if your roof and grid tariffs support it. A heat pump beats a gas boiler
on emissions in almost every grid in the world today, because it moves 3-4 units of heat per unit
of electricity.
